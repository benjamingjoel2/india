It is one of the most common choices we help travelers make, and one of the most enjoyable. Rajasthan and Kerala sit at opposite ends of the country and, in many ways, at opposite ends of the Indian experience. One is desert forts, royal palaces and a blaze of turbans and mirrorwork. The other is palm-lined backwaters, misty tea hills and the slow green rhythm of the tropics.

Neither is the "right" answer. The right one is the one that matches how you like to travel. Here is how to tell.

## The short version

- **Choose Rajasthan** if you love history, architecture, markets and a sense of theatre, and you are happy with a busier pace and more time on the road.
- **Choose Kerala** if you want to slow down, eat beautifully, spend time in nature and come home rested.
- **Choose both** if you have around three weeks. They pair surprisingly well, and a domestic flight links them in a few hours.

## Rajasthan: the land of kings

Rajasthan means, roughly, "land of kings", and the name is earned. For centuries this was a patchwork of Rajput kingdoms, each with its own fort, palace, court and style. What survives is extraordinary: hilltop citadels like Mehrangarh in Jodhpur and Kumbhalgarh with its immense walls, lake palaces in Udaipur, the honey-coloured fort city of Jaisalmer rising out of the Thar Desert.

### What a Rajasthan trip feels like

Days are full. Mornings are for forts and palaces before the heat builds, afternoons for bazaars, workshops and long lunches in shaded courtyards. Evenings might bring a rooftop dinner looking over the blue houses of Jodhpur or a quiet boat on Lake Pichola as the City Palace lights up.

Many of the best places to stay are heritage properties: havelis, hunting lodges and palaces, some still owned by the families who built them. Staying in them is part of the story, not just a bed for the night.

### The road between

Rajasthan is large and most travel is by road. Drives of four to six hours between cities are common. They are rarely dull, with camels on the highway, roadside dhabas and villages where women in bright odhnis carry water, but they are real time in the car. A good itinerary breaks them up with stops at places like Ranakpur's marble Jain temple or the stepwells of Abhaneri.

## Kerala: God's own country

Kerala's tourism slogan, "God's Own Country", sounds like marketing until you are there. A narrow strip between the Arabian Sea and the Western Ghats, the state is laced with rivers, lagoons and canals, and the green is almost overwhelming after rain.

### What a Kerala trip feels like

Slower, and deliberately so. A typical journey might begin in Kochi, with its fishing nets, spice markets and layered history of Arab, Chinese, Portuguese, Dutch and British traders. From there, travelers head into the hills at Munnar for tea estates and cool air, perhaps on to the spice gardens and forests of Thekkady, and then down to the backwaters around Alleppey and Kumarakom for a night or two on a houseboat.

Many finish on the coast at Marari or Varkala, or with a few days of Ayurveda at a good retreat. Distances are shorter than in Rajasthan, though hill roads are winding and slow.

### Culture in Kerala

It would be a mistake to think of Kerala as only scenery. This is the home of Kathakali dance-drama, with its astonishing painted faces, and of Kalaripayattu, one of the world's oldest martial arts. Temple festivals bring caparisoned elephants and thundering chenda drums. And the food, from appam and stew at breakfast to a banana-leaf sadya at lunch, is one of the great pleasures of the south.

## Comparing them side by side

### Best time to visit

Both are at their best from roughly October to March, but there are nuances.

- **Rajasthan** is lovely from October to March. December and January bring cold nights in the desert, and from April the heat builds quickly. The monsoon here is lighter than in the south, and July to September can be quiet and atmospheric, though hot and humid.
- **Kerala** is most comfortable from October to March, and drier from December. The southwest monsoon usually arrives in early June and brings heavy rain through the summer. It is also, traditionally, the favoured season for Ayurvedic treatments.

See our [month-by-month guide](/when-to-go) for more detail.

### Pace and energy

Rajasthan is stimulating and busy, with more sightseeing and more road time. Kerala is gentler, with more downtime built in. If this is your first trip to India and you are slightly nervous, Kerala is the softer landing. If you thrive on stimulation, Rajasthan will thrill you.

### Food

Rajasthan's cooking is rich and hearty, built on wheat, millet, ghee and dried pulses suited to the desert: dal baati churma, laal maas, gatte ki sabzi. Kerala's cooking is lighter, coastal and full of coconut, black pepper, curry leaf and fresh fish. Vegetarians eat superbly in both.

### Families and honeymooners

Both work well for families. Rajasthan's forts are a natural adventure for children, while Kerala's houseboats, beaches and gentle pace suit younger kids. For honeymooners, it comes down to style: Rajasthan offers palace suites and candlelit dinners on lake terraces, Kerala offers private pool villas, sunsets on the backwaters and the chance to do very little at all.

## Why not both?

If you have eighteen days or more, the combination is hard to beat. Begin in Delhi, travel through Agra and Rajasthan for ten or twelve days, then fly south from Jaipur or Udaipur, usually via a connection, and spend the final week in Kerala. You finish rested, which makes the flight home far easier, and you see two very different faces of the same country.

## Our honest advice

When travelers are undecided, we ask three questions. Do you want to come home with stories or come home rested? Do you prefer architecture or landscape? And how do you feel about long days on the road?

The answers almost always make the choice clear. And if they do not, that is usually a sign you should do both, or plan to come back.
