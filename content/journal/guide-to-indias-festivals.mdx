Some of the most vivid days you can spend in India are festival days. A whole city lit with clay lamps. Streets running pink and green with coloured powder. Drums rolling through the night as a goddess is carried to the river. These are not shows put on for visitors. They are living celebrations, and being welcomed into one is a privilege.

Here is our guide to the festivals we most love to plan around, with honest advice on timing, crowds and how to take part well.

## First, a word on dates

Most Indian festivals follow the Hindu lunisolar calendar, or other religious and regional calendars, so they move from year to year in the Western calendar. Diwali, for example, can fall anywhere from mid-October to mid-November. Some festivals are fixed by tradition to particular days, others are announced locally.

<Callout title="Always verify the dates">The months below are typical, not guaranteed. Festival dates follow lunar calendars and regional custom and shift every year. Check the current year's dates before you book flights, and if a festival is the reason for your trip, build in a day or two of flexibility.</Callout>

## Diwali: the festival of lights

**Typically October or November. Celebrated across India.**

Diwali is the most widely celebrated festival in the country. Homes are cleaned and decorated, rangoli patterns are drawn at doorways, and on the main night, clay diyas and electric lights glow from every window and rooftop. Families gather, sweets are exchanged and Lakshmi, goddess of prosperity, is welcomed into the home.

Rajasthan's cities are especially beautiful at Diwali, with Jaipur's bazaars strung with lights. Varanasi celebrates Dev Deepawali a couple of weeks later, when the ghats are lined with lamps.

**Good to know:** fireworks are loud and widespread, and air quality in North Indian cities can be poor in this season. Hotels book up early, and many Indians travel at this time too.

Read more in our [Diwali guide](/festivals/diwali).

## Holi: the festival of colours

**Typically March. Celebrated mostly in North India.**

Holi marks the arrival of spring and the triumph of good over evil. On the eve, bonfires are lit for Holika Dahan. The next morning, people take to the streets throwing coloured powder and water at friends, neighbours and strangers alike. By midday, everyone is a riot of pink, green and yellow.

The Braj region around Mathura and Vrindavan, linked to the life of Krishna, celebrates over many days, including the famous Lathmar Holi at Barsana. For many travelers, a quieter celebration in a heritage hotel or with a family is more enjoyable than the crowded public squares.

**Good to know:** wear old clothes, oil your hair and skin beforehand so the colour washes off, and protect your camera. Women travelers in particular may prefer a hosted celebration, as street crowds can become boisterous.

See our [Holi guide](/festivals/holi).

## Pushkar Camel Fair

**Typically October or November, around the full moon of Kartik. Pushkar, Rajasthan.**

Every year, the small holy town of Pushkar, around its sacred lake, fills with camel herders, livestock traders, pilgrims and travelers. The livestock trading happens mostly in the days before the full moon, when the dunes are dotted with camels and tented camps. As the full moon approaches, the emphasis shifts to pilgrimage, with devotees bathing in the lake.

**Good to know:** arrive in the early days for the camels and stay through for the spiritual atmosphere if you can. Tented camps and hotels sell out well in advance. Pushkar is a holy town, so alcohol and meat are not generally served.

## Durga Puja

**Typically September or October. Kolkata and West Bengal especially.**

Durga Puja is Kolkata's great festival, and one of the most astonishing artistic events anywhere. For several days, the city fills with thousands of pandals, temporary structures that range from traditional shrines to ambitious art installations, each housing a hand-sculpted image of the goddess Durga. The city stays up late, walking from pandal to pandal, eating street food and visiting friends. On the final day, the images are carried to the river for immersion.

Durga Puja in Kolkata was inscribed on UNESCO's list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2021.

**Good to know:** crowds are enormous in the evenings, so visit major pandals in the morning or very late at night. Hotels in Kolkata fill fast.

## Hornbill Festival

**Typically early December. Near Kohima, Nagaland.**

Named after the hornbill, a bird deeply woven into Naga culture, this festival brings together Nagaland's many tribes at the heritage village of Kisama near Kohima. Each tribe presents its traditional dances, songs, games, crafts and food. It is a rare chance to see a part of India few travelers reach, and to meet communities with distinct cultures and histories.

**Good to know:** accommodation near Kohima is limited, and homestays and camps fill months ahead. Check current entry requirements for foreign visitors to Nagaland before you travel, as permit rules have changed over the years.

## Hemis Festival

**Typically June or July. Hemis Monastery, Ladakh.**

Held at Hemis, one of Ladakh's largest monasteries, this festival celebrates the birth of Guru Padmasambhava, who is revered for bringing Tantric Buddhism to the Himalaya. The highlight is the cham, sacred masked dances performed by monks in vivid silk robes in the monastery courtyard, accompanied by long horns and drums. Every twelve years, a great thangka of Padmasambhava is unveiled.

**Good to know:** Ladakh sits at high altitude, so plan two or three easy days in Leh to acclimatise before you visit. The festival coincides with Ladakh's peak season, so book early.

## A few more worth knowing

- **Onam**, usually August or September, Kerala's harvest festival, with flower carpets, snake boat races and a grand vegetarian sadya feast.
- **Ganesh Chaturthi**, usually August or September, celebrated most exuberantly in Mumbai, with huge processions carrying images of Ganesh to the sea.
- **Navratri and Dussehra**, usually September or October, with nights of garba dancing in Gujarat and the burning of Ravana effigies across the north. Mysuru's Dasara is a highlight in the south.
- **Pongal**, in mid-January, Tamil Nadu's harvest festival, with sweet rice boiled until it overflows for good fortune.
- **Losar**, Tibetan New Year, celebrated in Ladakh, Sikkim and other Himalayan regions, with dates that vary by tradition.

## How to experience festivals respectfully

- **Remember it is not a performance.** Watch first, join when invited, and follow the lead of your hosts.
- **Ask before photographing people,** especially at prayer, and never photograph inside shrines where it is not allowed.
- **Dress modestly** and carry a scarf for temples and monasteries. Remove shoes where asked.
- **Accept food and sweets with your right hand.**
- **Mind the crowds.** Keep valuables secure, agree a meeting point with your group and avoid the densest moments if you are uneasy in crowds.

## Booking ahead

Festivals bring huge domestic travel as well as international visitors. For the most popular ones, Diwali in Rajasthan, the Pushkar fair, Durga Puja and Hornbill, we suggest booking four to six months in advance, or more if you want a particular hotel or camp.
