The first question almost everyone asks us is some version of "where do I even begin?" It is a fair question. India is a country of twenty-eight states, more than twenty officially recognized languages and a coastline that runs from desert salt flats to coconut-fringed lagoons. You will not see it all in one trip, and that is the good news: it means the first journey can be shaped around what you love, not around a checklist.

Here is how we think about a first visit, drawn from years of planning them and, more usefully, from the conversations we have with travelers when they come home.

## Start with a shape, not a list

The most rewarding first trips have a clear shape: one or two regions, a steady pace and enough time in each place to settle. Two weeks is a lovely length. Ten days works well if you keep the map small. Three weeks lets you add a second region without rushing.

What tends to go wrong is the opposite approach, where a traveler tries to fit the Taj Mahal, Kerala, Varanasi, Goa and the Himalaya into twelve days. Distances in India are real. Domestic flights are good, but airport days eat into your time, and the joy of the country often lives in the slower hours: a late breakfast in a haveli courtyard, an unplanned conversation with a textile printer, the light changing over a lake.

### A simple rule of thumb

- Allow at least two nights in most places, and three where there is a lot to see.
- Plan no more than one long transfer every two or three days.
- Leave one afternoon in every four completely free.

<Callout title="A practical note on pace">India rewards curiosity, and curiosity needs room. Free time is not wasted time. It is when many of our travelers say the trip really began.</Callout>

## The classic first trip: Delhi, Agra and Rajasthan

There is a reason the Golden Triangle of Delhi, Agra and Jaipur is the best-loved first route in the country. It is compact, well connected and astonishingly rich. In a little over a week you can move from the Mughal grandeur of Old Delhi to the Taj Mahal at sunrise and on to the pink-washed bazaars and hilltop forts of Jaipur.

In Delhi, give yourself time for both cities: the old one around Jama Masjid and Chandni Chowk, with its spice lanes and paratha shops, and the broad, tree-lined avenues of Lutyens' New Delhi. Humayun's Tomb, quieter than the Taj and its architectural ancestor, is one of our favorite first-morning visits.

In Agra, the Taj Mahal deserves its reputation. Go at first light, when the marble turns from gray to rose to white, and then visit Agra Fort and the garden of Mehtab Bagh across the river. The Taj is closed to general visitors on Fridays, so plan around it.

Jaipur is where many travelers fall for Rajasthan. Amber Fort, the City Palace, the observatory of Jantar Mantar and the block printers of nearby Sanganer are all worth your time. If you have a few more days, continue to Udaipur for its lakes and palaces, or to Jodhpur for the blue old town beneath Mehrangarh Fort.

## The gentler first trip: Kerala and the south

If the idea of crowds and big cities gives you pause, South India makes a wonderful introduction. Kerala in particular is easy to love. Start in Kochi, where Chinese fishing nets, Portuguese churches, Jewish synagogue lanes and spice warehouses sit side by side, then head up into the tea gardens of Munnar before drifting down to the backwaters on a kettuvallam houseboat.

The south moves at a different tempo. Food is lighter and full of coconut, curry leaf and fresh fish. Days are framed by the sound of temple bells and church choirs. Tamil Nadu, next door, adds some of the greatest temple architecture on Earth, at Madurai, Thanjavur and the shore temples of Mamallapuram.

## Adding a little wildness

A first trip can be more than monuments. Ranthambore, a few hours from Jaipur, is one of the best places in the world to see a wild tiger, and it slots neatly into a Rajasthan journey. Two or three nights there, with four to six safari drives, give you a real chance of a sighting and a glimpse of forest life well beyond the big cats. Our [tiger safari page](/experiences/tiger-safaris) explains how it works.

If the mountains call to you, the Himalaya is a different trip entirely, best kept for a second visit or a dedicated summer journey to Ladakh. See our [Himalaya guide](/where-to-go/himalaya).

## When to go

For most first-time routes, the most comfortable months are October to March, when the north is dry and pleasantly warm by day and cool at night. December and January can be foggy in Delhi and Agra in the early morning, and chilly enough in the desert to want a jacket after dark.

From April to June the plains are very hot, though this is a good time for the mountains. The southwest monsoon generally arrives in Kerala around early June and spreads north over the following weeks. Monsoon travel can be beautiful, green and quiet, but it suits travelers who are happy to be flexible. Our [month-by-month guide](/when-to-go) goes into detail.

<Callout title="Festivals change the picture">Diwali, Holi and the Pushkar Camel Fair can be highlights of a first trip, but they follow lunar calendars and move each year. Hotels fill quickly around them. Check this year's dates before you fix your travel window.</Callout>

## What to expect, honestly

India can be a lot at first, and it is better to say so. Traffic is busy. Cities are loud. Some days will be long. But the country is also deeply hospitable, and with a good plan and a good guide, the noise quickly becomes texture rather than stress.

A few things our travelers wish they had known:

- **Dress for temples.** Carry a light scarf or shawl and wear clothes that cover shoulders and knees. You will remove your shoes at most religious sites.
- **Eat well, eat sensibly.** Freshly cooked, hot food is usually the safest choice. Drink bottled or filtered water. You do not need to avoid street food, but let your guide point you to the right stalls.
- **Carry small notes.** Tipping is customary, and change is often scarce.
- **Expect warmth.** People will ask where you are from, whether you are married and what you think of India. It is curiosity, not intrusion.

## Why a good guide changes everything

The difference between seeing India and understanding it often comes down to the person beside you. A good guide does not just recite dates. They know which chai stall makes the best cup in the old city, when the light is best on the fort walls and which family has been weaving the same pattern for five generations. They also smooth the practical edges, so you can spend your energy on being there.

That is the heart of how we plan. Every journey we build is shaped around the traveler, with guides and hosts we know personally, and room in the days for the moments no itinerary can promise.

## Where to begin

If you are still deciding, here is our honest advice. If you want the iconic sights and a strong sense of history, start in the north with Delhi, Agra and Rajasthan. If you want a slower, softer introduction, start in Kerala and Tamil Nadu. If you have three weeks, you can do a taste of both, flying south for the final week to unwind.
