There is a moment that every safari traveler remembers. The jeep goes quiet. A langur barks from high in a tree, a chital deer stamps and calls, and the naturalist raises one hand without turning around. Then, out of the dry grass or along a dusty forest track, a tiger simply appears, walking as if you are not there at all.

India is home to the majority of the world's wild tigers, and seeing one is entirely possible with the right planning. Here is what we have learned from years of sending travelers into the forests of Central and North India.

## Where to go: the best tiger parks

India has more than fifty tiger reserves, but a handful stand out for travelers, thanks to healthy tiger populations, good lodges and experienced guides.

### Ranthambore, Rajasthan

The most accessible of the great parks, a few hours from Jaipur and easy to add to a Rajasthan journey. Ranthambore's dry deciduous forest is scattered with lakes, ruined pavilions and a vast tenth-century fort, which gives sightings here a particularly cinematic backdrop. It is also the busiest park, so careful zone choice matters.

### Bandhavgarh, Madhya Pradesh

Long celebrated for one of the highest densities of tigers in India. The landscape of sal forest, grassy meadows and rocky hills around an ancient fort is beautiful, and sightings are frequent in a good season.

### Kanha, Madhya Pradesh

Vast, green and atmospheric, with sal forest and sweeping meadows that inspired Kipling's Jungle Book. Kanha is also famous for its conservation of the hard-ground barasingha, a swamp deer brought back from the brink. It rewards travelers who want the whole forest, not just the cat.

### Tadoba-Andhari, Maharashtra

Tadoba has earned a strong reputation in recent years for excellent sightings, especially in the hotter months. Its teak and bamboo forest is less visited by international travelers than the Madhya Pradesh parks, and it is reachable from Nagpur.

### Also worth considering

- **Pench**, on the Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra border, is lovely, relatively quiet and pairs well with Kanha.
- **Jim Corbett**, in the Himalayan foothills of Uttarakhand, is India's oldest national park, with river landscapes, elephants and some of the best birding in the north. Tiger sightings are less predictable than in Central India, but the scenery is superb.

## When to go

Most Indian tiger reserves open their core zones from around October to June and close them during the monsoon, typically from July to September, when tracks become impassable and animals breed. Some buffer zones stay open year-round.

Within the season, there is a trade-off.

- **October to February** is green, cool and very pleasant. Mornings can be cold in an open jeep, especially in December and January. The forest is lush, which can make tigers harder to spot.
- **March to June** is hot, sometimes very hot, with temperatures that can climb well above 40°C by May. But the vegetation thins out and water becomes scarce, so tigers come to waterholes and are easier to see. Many experienced wildlife travelers choose April or May for this reason.

<Callout title="Verify seasons and dates">Opening and closing dates, safari timings and weekly closures vary by park and state and can change from year to year. Some parks close for one afternoon a week or on certain holidays. Always check the current rules before fixing your dates.</Callout>

## Zones, permits and booking

Each park is divided into zones, and permits are issued for a specific zone, date and time slot, morning or afternoon. Numbers are strictly limited to protect the forest, and the best zones sell out early.

Permits are sold through official state forest department portals, and for popular parks they usually open months in advance. For peak dates, such as Christmas, New Year and Indian school holidays, it is wise to book as soon as the window opens. Rules, prices and release windows change, so treat any figure you read online with caution and check the official source.

We handle permits for our travelers, but it is helpful to understand that a safari plan is built around availability. Flexibility on zones gives you the best chance.

## Jeep or canter?

Safaris are run in two kinds of vehicle.

- **Jeeps**, open-topped 4x4s usually seating six guests, plus a driver and a forest guide. They are more nimble, quieter and give everyone a good view. Most travelers prefer them.
- **Canters**, larger open-sided trucks seating around twenty. They are cheaper and easier to book at short notice, but noisier, less flexible and less comfortable for photography.

If you can, book a jeep. If you are traveling as a couple or a family, booking the whole vehicle gives you control over pace and space.

## How many safaris should you plan?

This is the single most important piece of advice we can give. Tigers are wild, and there are no guarantees. One or two drives may be lucky. They may not.

We suggest at least five or six safaris, spread over three nights or more, and ideally two parks on a longer trip. That gives the forest time to reveal itself, and it means a quiet drive is part of the rhythm rather than a disappointment.

> The travelers who see the most are rarely the luckiest. They are the ones who gave the forest enough time.

## The difference a naturalist makes

Every vehicle carries a forest department guide, which is required. But a good lodge naturalist, who often joins as well, turns a safari from a search into an education. They read pugmarks in the dust, know the territories of individual tigers and listen for alarm calls from deer, langurs and peafowl. They also explain the forest when the tiger stays hidden, which is often when the best learning happens.

## Safari ethics: how to watch well

Tiger tourism can support conservation and local livelihoods, but only if it is done well. These are the standards we hold to and ask of our travelers.

- **No baiting, no chasing.** Never support any operator that lures animals or pursues them.
- **No crowding.** If several vehicles gather, keep a respectful distance and do not push for a closer position.
- **Stay quiet.** Speak in whispers, keep phones on silent and never call out to an animal.
- **Stay in the vehicle.** Getting out is forbidden and dangerous.
- **Follow the rules.** Speed limits, timings and route restrictions exist for good reason. A good guide will not bend them, and you should not ask.
- **No flash photography** and no littering.

## What to wear and pack

- Neutral colors: khaki, olive, brown and beige. Avoid white and bright colors.
- Layers for winter mornings, when an open jeep at dawn can be genuinely cold: a warm jacket, hat and gloves. Many lodges provide blankets and hot water bottles.
- A scarf or buff against dust, which can be heavy in dry months.
- A wide-brimmed hat, sunscreen and sunglasses for hot-season drives.
- Binoculars, ideally one pair per person.
- A camera with a good zoom. A 100–400mm or similar range is ideal.

## More than tigers

The tiger may be the reason you come, but it is not the whole story. These forests hold leopards, which are often seen on rocky outcrops, sloth bears shuffling along at dusk, packs of dhole, the Indian wild dog, gaur, the world's largest wild cattle, and herds of sambar and chital.

The birdlife is wonderful too. Look for crested serpent eagles, paradise flycatchers trailing white ribbons of tail, the electric blue of the Indian roller and the haunting call of the peacock at dawn.
